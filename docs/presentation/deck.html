Efficient proof of training steps

Pacif.ai

• Topic: how an independent auditor can confirm that an AI model was trained exactly as claimed, on the agreed data, step by step. • Why it matters: a trainer could secretly slip harmful data (a "backdoor") into training. Today, checking for that is expensive or only statistical. • The promise of this talk: a check that covers every step and the whole model, gives a mathematical guarantee, and costs the auditor far less than re-training.

A global check on first and last steps

• Two roles. The prover is whoever trains the model. The verifier is an independent auditor. • Before training starts, both sides agree on the starting model, the dataset, and which records go into each training step, in order. • Steps 1–2: the prover loads the agreed batch and runs one full training step: forward pass, loss, backward pass, optimizer update. • Step 3: the prover records the final weights and every intermediate matrix-multiplication result. • Step 4: the record is hashed into a Merkle tree. The root hash commits to every value: change one number and the root changes. • Step 5: the verifier recomputes the Merkle root and checks it matches. • Step 6: the verifier validates each multiplication with random-vector checks instead of redoing it (7 vectors per multiplication at test scale). The random challenges are derived from the root hash (Fiat–Shamir), so the prover can't know them before committing and no live back-and-forth is needed. Each check costs n², not n³. • Step 7: the batch must be the agreed one, the weight change must follow the training rule exactly, and each step must start where the last one ended. • Steps are checked one at a time, so the verifier's memory stays flat no matter how long training runs. • Outcome: pass moves to the next step; fail rejects the run. Before step 1 the starting model must be the agreed base model; after the last step the final model must be the one actually deployed.

• Left: the protocol from the previous slide. Right: each red arrow is an attack, pointing at the verifier check that rejects it. • Edit the record → step 5. The root hash commits to every value, and the random checks are derived from it. Editing anything afterwards changes the hash, so the old answers no longer fit. • Fake a calculation → step 6: a faked multiplication fails the random-vector checks. The same check catches reporting one batch while training on another, since the verifier rebuilds each step's inputs from the reported batch. • Retrying until the checks look lucky (grinding) is also covered by step 6: we use enough checks to survive about 2^52 retries, roughly what a well-funded research lab could afford. • Tamper with weights → step 7: the weight change is checked number by number against the training rule. • Use unagreed data → step 7: every record in a batch is checked against the agreed dataset's hash at its scheduled position. Step 7 also catches skipped, swapped or reordered steps, since each step must start exactly where the last one ended. • Swap the model → the global check: the first model must be the agreed base model and the last must be the one actually deployed. • Bottom line: a cheating run gets through with probability at most about 1 in 30 million (2^-25), even counting retries. • Not covered, by design: we prove the agreed recipe was followed on the agreed data. If the agreed data or recipe is itself bad, honest training on it passes. Very small changes (below a floor set by rounding noise and the number of checks) aren't guaranteed to be caught.

• Three ways to check that training was done honestly. • Proof of Learning (Jia et al., 2021): the trainer saves checkpoints, and the auditor re-runs selected stretches of training and checks the result lands close by. The auditor pays full training cost for whatever it checks, and later papers showed that forged training logs can pass the distance test. • PoTS (Seddik et al., 2025): every step, the auditor freezes the early layers, re-trains only the last few layers, and accepts if the result is within a statistical threshold. That still needs a forward pass and partial training every step. It only looks at the tail of the model, and the guarantee is statistical. • Ours: no re-training at all. Every multiplication in every step is spot-checked against random vectors, across the whole model. • Why it's cheaper: redoing a multiplication of size n costs about n³ operations, while spot-checking it costs about n². For the typical layer shapes of our test model, 7 spot-checks are expected to cost only a few percent of redoing the multiplication. The non-multiplication steps are recomputed, but they're small. • The bars show the expected ordering, not measured values. The measured comparison goes on the results slide. • Backdoors: Proof of Learning doesn't protect against backdoors; PoTS and our protocol do. • Honest trade-off, for Q&A: the trainer has to record and send every multiplication result, so the prover side carries more storage and bandwidth.
